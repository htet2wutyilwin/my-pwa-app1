01 · History

A drink shaped by migration, trade, religion, science, and agriculture

Archaeological evidence places very early winemaking traditions in the South Caucasus and surrounding parts of western Asia thousands of years ago. As viticulture spread, wine became part of trade networks and ceremonial life around the Mediterranean.

Greek and Roman agriculture expanded vineyard knowledge; monasteries later preserved and refined many European vineyard traditions. From the early modern period onward, colonial trade and migration carried grapevines to the Americas, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand.

Modern wine reflects both old regional customs and newer advances in microbiology, temperature control, vineyard mapping, and climate science.

02 · Regions

Major wine-producing regions

Regional identity comes from climate, soils, grape choices, farming traditions, and local rules. These examples offer a starting point rather than a complete map.

France

Bordeaux & Burgundy

Bordeaux is associated with Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot blends; Burgundy is strongly linked with Pinot Noir and Chardonnay.

Italy

Tuscany & Piedmont

Tuscany is closely tied to Sangiovese, while Piedmont is known for Nebbiolo and a long tradition of site-specific wines.

Spain

Rioja & Ribera del Duero

Tempranillo is central to both regions, with differences in climate, elevation, oak traditions, and local classification.

Germany

Mosel & Rheingau

Cool conditions, river valleys, and steep vineyard sites have helped Riesling become a defining grape.

United States

California & Oregon

California spans warm and cool growing zones; Oregon’s Willamette Valley is especially associated with Pinot Noir.

South America

Argentina & Chile

High-altitude Argentine vineyards and Chile’s long north-south geography create a wide range of growing conditions.

South Africa

Cape Winelands

Mediterranean influences, mountain ranges, and coastal weather shape a historic industry centered around the Western Cape.

Australia & New Zealand

Southern Hemisphere

Australia is highly diverse in climate and grapes; New Zealand is particularly known for cool-climate regions and Sauvignon Blanc.

03 · Grapes

Eight influential grape varieties

Grape variety influences aroma, acidity, tannin, color, and structure, but climate and production choices can change the final profile significantly.

Cabernet Sauvignon

Thick-skinned red grape often associated with firm tannins and dark-fruit aromas.

Merlot

Red variety often described as rounder in texture, with plum and herbal notes depending on climate.

Pinot Noir

Thin-skinned red grape that thrives in many cooler regions and can show red-fruit, floral, and earthy characteristics.

Syrah / Shiraz

A red grape whose styles vary widely, from peppery and savory to ripe and fruit-forward.

Chardonnay

A highly adaptable white grape; climate, malolactic fermentation, lees contact, and oak can strongly shape its style.

Sauvignon Blanc

White variety often known for high acidity and citrus, herbal, or tropical aromas depending on growing conditions.

Riesling

Aromatic white grape with naturally high acidity and styles that range from dry to sweet.

Tempranillo

Spanish red variety associated with red and dark fruit, savory notes, and a strong affinity for oak aging traditions.

04 · Categories

How wine is commonly grouped

StillNon-sparkling wines, including red, white, and rosé.
SparklingWines containing dissolved carbon dioxide from fermentation or other permitted methods.
FortifiedWines whose production includes the addition of grape spirit, a historic preservation technique.
SweetWines with notable residual sugar, produced through several different vineyard and cellar methods.

05 · Vineyard & cellar

Understanding wine production

Wine production is studied through vineyard science, fermentation biology, maturation, and regional tradition. Methods vary widely by place and style.

  1. 1

    Vineyard season

    Viticulture studies how vine health, climate, soil, water, and canopy conditions influence grape development.

  2. 2

    Harvest decisions

    Harvest timing reflects grape maturity, acidity, flavor development, weather, and local tradition.

  3. 3

    Fruit handling

    Winemaking traditions differ in how grapes, juice, skins, and solids are handled before and during fermentation.

  4. 4

    Fermentation science

    Fermentation is a biological process in which yeast transforms grape sugars and produces carbon dioxide along with many aroma compounds.

  5. 5

    Maturation

    Different vessel materials—such as steel, concrete, oak, or clay—can influence oxygen exposure, texture, and aroma.

  6. 6

    Finishing & bottling

    Before bottling, producers may use clarification, stabilization, filtration, or blending depending on style and regional practice.

06 · Global culture

Wine as cultural heritage

Across many societies, vineyards are part of rural landscapes, seasonal labor, cuisine, festivals, trade, and family histories. Wine terminology often preserves local geography and languages.

Appellation systems in many countries connect wine names to origin and production rules. Elsewhere, labels may emphasize grape variety, producer, or broader geographic identity.

Studying wine culture can reveal how agriculture, climate, law, economics, religion, migration, and craft intersect over time.

07 · Glossary

Essential wine terminology

Appellation
A legally or historically defined geographic origin for wine, often with production rules.
Terroir
A concept linking a wine’s character to site, including climate, soil, topography, and human tradition.
Vintage
The year in which the grapes used for a wine were harvested.
Tannin
Phenolic compounds, especially important in red wines, that contribute structure and a drying sensation.
Acidity
Naturally occurring acids that contribute freshness, balance, and stability.
Body
A general description of perceived weight or fullness on the palate.
Lees
Yeast and other particles that settle after fermentation; some wines are matured in contact with them.
Malolactic fermentation
A microbial conversion of sharper malic acid into softer lactic acid, common in many styles.
Oak aging
Maturation in oak vessels, which can affect texture, oxygen exposure, and aroma.
Blend
A wine made by combining different grape varieties, vineyard lots, regions, or batches.
Residual sugar
Grape sugar remaining in wine after fermentation stops or is stopped.
Phylloxera
A vine pest that devastated many vineyards in the 19th century and changed global viticulture.
